## Summary
Jefferson Fisher interviews former FBI hostage negotiator Chris Voss about tactical empathy tools from *Never Split the Difference*. Voss explains why steering people toward "no" makes them feel safe and open, how accusation audits and labels defuse negatives and draw out the truth, and why a slow, downward-inflected voice helps connection stick. He closes by warning that needing to win every argument slowly drives people away, and Fisher recaps three takeaways.

## Ideas
### Fear voice versus gut
Voss says most of the voices in your head come from the amygdala, the fear centre, which was useful against saber-tooth tigers but now makes you paranoid and overreactive. The task is sorting fear from intuition, and he sometimes asks himself "what is my gut telling me?" Tiredness makes the amygdala more likely to take the controls, while the gut is "really accurate."
By: Chris Voss | [02:46]

### "No" makes people feel safe
The person who says "no" feels protected, so they become more open to dialogue and listening. A no is therefore the start of a conversation, because you stop being a threat. Voss and his son did not realise how significant this was when they first wrote the book.
By: Chris Voss | [04:27]

### Why kids say no
Voss argues children copy what they see parents do. Kids hear "no" from parents all the time, so they learn that saying it asserts autonomy and control, and it feels good to say.
By: Chris Voss | [06:41]

### Yes-momentum has made "yes" feel like a trap
Sales tactics got people to give a string of small agreements (more money, bigger house) and then led them down a path. After being trapped repeatedly, people develop a Pavlovian suspicion of being steered to "yes". The opposite is therefore true: people relax when they can say "no."
By: Chris Voss | [07:16]

### Hospital sales example shows flipping works
See Stories. The idea behind it is that people condition themselves so that saying "no" feels safe, and then they entertain things they were blocking because "yes" scared them.
By: Chris Voss | [10:04]

### Start the question with a negative
Fisher summarises the technique: open the sentence with a negative word so the natural answer is also negative. Examples are "would it be terrible of me to ask", "would you be offended by", and "is now a bad time". The info sought is the same, but the "no" is comfortable to give. Voss confirms this is perfectly described.
By: Jefferson Fisher, confirmed by Chris Voss | [16:12]

### Ask "bad time to talk" to get undivided attention
"Is now a bad time to talk" gets a brief hesitation, then either "no, what do you got" or a real alternative time. It stops people multitasking and lowers their guard. Voss says he has never had anyone refuse to give him a better time.
By: Chris Voss | [11:43]

### Warning someone of bad news makes them imagine worse
Voss notes that when you prepare someone for bad news, the worst that happens is they imagine something equal to what you have to say, and nine times out of ten they imagine worse. That helped his collaborator arrive focused and ready to solve the problem.
By: Chris Voss | [14:30]

### Accusation audit
Think like an accountant auditing the negatives the other side may be holding against you, be liberal and creative, and say them out loud up front. Labeling a negative defuses it, and Voss says brain science backs this. You can't plant emotions in someone's head, only work with what is already there.
By: Chris Voss | [17:53]

### State the accusation as a prediction, not a denial
Saying "I don't want to sound disrespectful" is a denial and wrong. "I'm going to sound disrespectful" is a prediction and an observation, so it deactivates the issue. Voss says to use the strong word and not pull punches.
By: Chris Voss | [18:29]

### Accusation audits work because people love to correct
Fisher suggests the contrarian streak explains why audits work. Voss agrees it is two or three things, including correcting, as in "I don't feel that way." The Judge Judy anecdote illustrates how people rush to correct.
By: Jefferson Fisher and Chris Voss | [22:25]

### What's behind a statement matters more than the statement
Voss says what is behind what someone said is always more important than what they said, the "question behind the question." His claim is that your guess about their thinking is right about 30% of the time, so you need to draw it out.
By: Chris Voss | [24:39]

### Statement beats "What makes you say that?"
The word "what" makes someone stop and think in depth, which is tiring, and they may not have the energy to answer or will polish the answer. A statement like "it seems like you have a reason for saying that" is easier, feels open and not judgmental, and gets a straight, unvarnished answer.
By: Chris Voss | [25:12]

### Don't exhaust a witness in deposition
Fisher says "sounds like there's more to that" works in depositions. Voss adds that a deposition makes you a threat by definition, and exhausting someone for hours reduces the information they can supply.
By: Jefferson Fisher and Chris Voss | [27:57]

### Passive-aggressive people: first decide whether to deal with them at all
Voss says "best chance of success" means no guarantee, so first ask if you have to deal with the person and whether you want to be handcuffed to them long-term. Past behaviour is the best indicator of future behaviour, and for him a passive-aggressive person is probably out. If you must stay, he would start labeling.
By: Chris Voss | [28:30]

### HALFs
Joe P calls people who are hard, annoying, lame and frustrating "HALFs" and says to cut them out of your life. Voss uses this as part of deciding whether to engage.
By: Chris Voss, crediting Joe P | [29:04]

### Passive aggression exhausts you
Passive-aggressive people throw it back with "what do you want me to do?" right after you've said what you want, which wears you out. Voss might label it with "it sounds to me like what I just said was confusing."
By: Chris Voss | [30:12]

### Labels are verbal observations of what you sense
A label starts with "it seems like," "it sounds like," "it looks like," or "it feels like," followed by an observation of an emotion or dynamic you see or sense. Voss says it is collaborative, open to correction and not accusatory, and if someone objects, you reply that you only said how it looked or sounded.
By: Chris Voss | [30:45]

### Labeling invites correction and truth
Fisher notes that people correct a label ("no, I'm not uneasy, I just...") and so give you the truth instead of you skating around it.
By: Jefferson Fisher | [32:23]

### Slow, low-energy voice makes connection stick
High volume, many words and high energy create connection with a very fast half-life. A calm voice resonates, makes people feel not pushed, and connection lasts. Voss learned it on a suicide hotline, then as a hostage negotiator, and a therapist told him it is how people are relaxed.
By: Chris Voss | [34:04]

### Downward inflection and a smile
Voss recommends downward inflection and slowing down, with an occasional smile for warmth. He says it does not need a deep voice and that women can downward inflect by dropping the chin.
By: Chris Voss | [35:11]

### Picture a voice as a music style
Fisher says he imagines a CD album for a person's voice, such as Sinatra, hip-hop or loud, to gauge their vibe. Slowing down makes you easier to listen to.
By: Jefferson Fisher | [35:44]

### Needing to win drives people away (slot machine effect)
Voss says someone who needs to win every argument will find conversations getting fewer and farther apart, and the wins obscure the losses. He compares it to slot machines, saying he read a machine pays out about 1 in 84 pulls, so the celebration hides losing 83 of 84. People end up paying an "annoyance tax" or avoiding you while agreeable people do better.
By: Chris Voss | [37:27]

## Frameworks
### Accusation audit
Use when: opening a negotiation or tense conversation, to break down barriers and get to the heart of the matter fast.
Steps:
1. Audit and inventory the negative thoughts the other side may hold about you, and be liberal about it.
2. State them out loud up front, as a prediction or observation and not a denial.
3. Use the strong word, and do it playfully on small stakes to get data.
4. Let them correct you and tell you more.
Shape: steps
By: Chris Voss | [17:53]

### Labeling
Use when: you see or sense an emotion or dynamic, such as foot-dragging, nervousness or confusion.
Steps:
1. Notice the emotion or dynamic.
2. Begin with "it seems like," "it sounds like," "it looks like" or "it feels like."
3. Say your observation out loud.
4. Accept correction without defensiveness.
Shape: steps
By: Chris Voss | [30:45]

### Flip the question to get a "no"
Use when: you want someone to open up and agree to talk or move forward without feeling pushed.
Steps:
1. Replace the "yes" question with a negative-opening version.
2. Ask it, for example "is now a bad time to talk?"
3. Take their "no" as safety and continue, or take the alternative time they give.
Shape: versus
By: Chris Voss, summarised by Jefferson Fisher | [11:43]

### HALF test
Use when: deciding whether to engage with a difficult person.
Steps:
1. Ask yourself whether you have to deal with this person.
2. Ask whether you want to be handcuffed to them long-term.
3. If not, cut them out; if you must stay, start labeling.
Shape: funnel
By: Chris Voss, crediting Joe P | [29:04]

## Applications
- Booking a hard call with a colleague or partner: open with "is now a bad time to talk" so you get an undivided time, using Flip the question to get a "no" [11:43].
- Asking a favour or raising a touchy topic at home: prefix with an accusation audit such as "you're going to hate me for this" [20:11].
- A team member gives a short or odd answer in a meeting: use "it sounds like you have a reason for saying that" and not "what makes you say that" [24:39].
- A coworker is dragging their feet or giving "what do you want me to do?": label it ("it feels like you're dragging your feet") and decide with the HALF test whether to engage [29:04, 30:12].
- A stalled sales or stakeholder deal: ask "are you against..." so the "no" lets them relax [09:31].
- Heated disagreement where you want to win: remember the slot machine effect and weigh the long-term relationship cost [37:27].
- Giving feedback or presenting under stress: slow down, downward-inflect, and smile [35:11].
- Interviewing or questioning someone: use statements and labels, not tiring "what" questions [27:57].
