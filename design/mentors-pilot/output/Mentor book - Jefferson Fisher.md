# Jefferson Fisher

## At a glance
Jefferson Fisher hosts The Jefferson Fisher Podcast, wrote the book The Next Conversation, runs a School of Communication, and says he cross-examines people in depositions. He teaches short, specific ways to answer rudeness with a question, a label or a pause, to say no first without excuses, and to leave other people's disappointment with them. None of it is backed by cited research in these four videos, and one of them is an interview in which he draws out tactical empathy from Chris Voss.

### Core principles
- **Kind, not nice.** Say the true thing with care, because nice is surface and kind is honest, a thread in 3 of 4 videos (rude comments answered with kindness in S1, kind versus nice in S2, kindness inside the no in S3). (S2 07:22)
- **No first, no excuses.** Lead with the decline, keep reasons and apologies short or absent, and give a reason only when asked by someone who matters, in 3 of 4 videos. (S2 15:42)
- **Check before you assume.** Ask what they intended or whether there is more, instead of guessing or retaliating, in 2 of 4. (S1 04:25)
- **Their feelings are theirs.** Disappointment and other reactions belong to the other person, so you do not manage or fear them, in 2 of 4. (S3 03:44)
- **Decide before you speak.** Your gut has already answered a request, and you expect the disappointment in advance, in 2 of 4. (S2 14:37)
- **Let silence do the work.** About four seconds answers a rude remark, and holding the silence after a no protects the decision, in 2 of 4. (S1 08:22)
- **Rudeness back only confirms it.** Trading rude comments is a losing game, in 1 of 4, with the guest's version of the same warning in S4. (S1 02:14)

## Chapters
### Ask Did You Mean
Playbook: [[Playbooks/Ask Did You Mean]]
Summary: When someone is rude or curt, Fisher starts the reply with did you mean, which turns an insult-sized guess into a question about intent. He applies it to rude comments, one-word texts and short emails.
Takeaways:
- The question gives the other person a chance to clarify and re-assess, and he says nine times out of ten they backtrack or explain, with no data given.
- Checking intent instead of assuming it makes you look like the person with integrity.
- In email, ask one quick question or ask briefly for more context instead of writing a long guess about their tone.
Diagram:
shape: steps
title: Did you mean
nodes:
  - Hold the comeback | answering rude with rude confirms what they said
  - Ask did you mean | name the effect in a few words
  - Wait | let them clarify or backtrack
  - Accept or ask for context | keep a short follow-up in email
Clip: S1 04:25 | He explains that starting with did you mean makes the other person double back on their words.

### Four-Second Silence
Playbook: [[Playbooks/Four-Second Silence]]
Summary: For a comment not worth engaging, Fisher holds about four seconds of silence with a look of mild puzzlement and moves on. He pictures the remark as a paper set aside unread on a counsel table.
Takeaways:
- The pause says you heard it, evaluated it and concluded it does not merit a reply.
- The counsel table image turns setting the remark aside into an expression of knowing your truth, presence and purpose.
- The notes give no guidance on when silence might read as hostile or avoidant.
Diagram:
shape: steps
title: Four seconds, then on
nodes:
  - Look | no words, mild puzzlement
  - Count to four | counting is optional
  - Set it on the counsel table | a paper set aside unread
  - Move on | carry on with your day
Clip: S1 09:34 | He describes the counsel desk beside him and the remark as paper he places on it without reading.

### Label What You Sense
Playbook: [[Playbooks/Label What You Sense]]
Summary: Say what you see or sense in a statement that starts with it sounds like, so the other person corrects you and tells you more. Fisher uses it on passive-aggressive remarks and in depositions, and Chris Voss teaches the full method as labeling.
Takeaways:
- Passive-aggressive people speak indirectly, and a gentle it-sounds-like prompt pulls them out of that mode.
- Voss says a statement beats a what question because in-depth thinking is tiring, and Fisher notes people correct a label and give you the truth.
- Before labeling a passive-aggressive person, Voss says to ask whether you have to deal with them at all.
Diagram:
shape: steps
title: Label it
nodes:
  - Decide if it is worth it | do you have to deal with this person
  - Notice | the emotion or dynamic you see or sense
  - Open with it sounds like | or it seems, looks or feels like
  - Say it as a statement | not a what question
  - Take the correction | no defensiveness
Clip: S4 30:45 | Voss defines a label as a verbal observation of what you sense that is collaborative and open to correction.

### Stand Your Ground and Sound Nice
Playbook: [[Playbooks/Stand Your Ground and Sound Nice]]
Summary: Fisher says nice gets you steamrolled and that nice is a tone, so you can sound nice and still say no, disagree or end the conversation. He gives a bank of phrases for pushing back, boundaries and disagreement, plus real-talk lead-ins.
Takeaways:
- Nice is surface and harmony, kind is depth and honesty, and someone can be both.
- You can sound nice and still stand your ground, and sometimes you should sound assertive instead.
- It is okay to please people as long as you are one of them.
Diagram:
shape: versus
title: Nice and kind
nodes:
  - Nice | Kind
  - Surface | Depth
  - Harmony | Honesty
  - Gets you steamrolled | Lets you stay real
  - Compliments your socks | Delivers the hard truth
Clip: S2 05:04 | He sets nice as surface and kind as depth, tying kind to the root kin.

### Say No First Without Excuses
Playbook: [[Playbooks/Say No First Without Excuses]]
Summary: Fisher says your gut has already answered a request, so the decline goes first, with thanks and a little kindness and no justification unless one is asked for and earned. In a message he names the disappointment instead of apologizing at length.
Takeaways:
- The decision is made before you speak, and you control only how you say it.
- Justifications are over nice, and a short true reason is fine for a close friend who understands.
- After the no, hold the silence instead of backfilling with softeners.
Diagram:
shape: steps
title: The three-part no
nodes:
  - The no | I can't be there, with no excuse
  - Gratitude | thanks for inviting you and thinking of you
  - A little kindness | tell me how it went
  - Let it go | they handle the disappointment
  - A reason only if asked | short, true, for someone who matters
Clip: S3 18:20 | He lays out the three-part no for invitations and points to an earlier episode on saying no.

### Let Them Be Disappointed
Playbook: [[Playbooks/Let Them Be Disappointed]]
Summary: Fisher argues disappointing people is a skill, because the fear is the feeling after the no and that feeling belongs to the other person. He gives leaders a rule to acknowledge frustration without fixing it, and a way to practice on small refusals.
Takeaways:
- You are not that important, and the imagined devastation is often in your head.
- Over-protecting people implies they are fragile and leaves them on crutches.
- Acknowledge the feeling and do not offer fixes, or you keep them on training wheels.
Diagram:
shape: versus
title: The no and the feeling after
nodes:
  - The no | The feeling after
  - Yours to say plainly | Theirs to feel
  - Name the disappointment | Do not fix it
  - Practice on small refusals | Then the big ones
Clip: S3 02:08 | He says people fear the feeling that follows a no and invent a parade of horrors to avoid it.

### Flip the Question to Get a No
Playbook: [[Playbooks/Flip the Question to Get a No]]
Summary: Chris Voss teaches that a no makes people feel safe and opens them to dialogue, so he asks questions whose comfortable answer is no. Fisher summarises the technique as opening the sentence with a negative word, and Voss confirms it.
Takeaways:
- Is now a bad time to talk gets a brief hesitation and then a real alternative time or a go-ahead.
- Voss says yes-momentum has made yes feel like a trap, so people relax when they can say no.
- Warning someone of bad news helps because they imagine worse than what you have to say.
Diagram:
shape: versus
title: Yes question, no question
nodes:
  - Yes question | No question
  - have you got a few minutes to talk | is now a bad time to talk
  - A yes-oriented pitch | are you against implementing this program
  - They feel pushed | They feel safe
Clip: S4 11:43 | Voss explains that asking whether now is a bad time to talk stops multitasking and lowers the guard.

### Accusation Audit
Playbook: [[Playbooks/Accusation Audit]]
Summary: Chris Voss says to audit the negatives the other side may hold about you and say them out loud up front, as a prediction and not a denial. Fisher suggests it works partly because people love to correct, and Voss agrees.
Takeaways:
- Saying I'm going to sound disrespectful deactivates the issue, where I don't want to sound disrespectful is a denial.
- You can't plant emotions in people's heads, so you can only work with what is already there.
- Voss's staff open with you're going to hate me for this before a small ask, which makes him imagine something horrible and then feel relieved.
Diagram:
shape: steps
title: Audit, state, correct
nodes:
  - Audit | list the negatives they may hold about you
  - State them first | out loud, up front
  - Predict, do not deny | I'm going to sound disrespectful
  - Use the strong word | do not pull punches
  - Let them correct you | and tell you more
Clip: S4 18:29 | Voss separates a denial from a prediction and says to use the strong word.
